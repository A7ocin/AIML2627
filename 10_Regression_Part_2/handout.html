From fitted predictions to uncertainty and interpretation

Regression predicts a numerical response from one or more inputs. Begin with the observed pairs, specify a candidate mean function, choose its coefficients by least squares and evaluate its predictions. Then allow polynomial features to represent curvature.

Part 1 follows linear regression, training, model evaluation and polynomial regression. Part 2 continues with bias–variance, accuracy of estimates, multiple regression and assumptions. The notation and examples below make each step explicit.

Five metrics: what does each one tell me?

A residual is an observed response minus the model’s prediction at the same input. RSS, the residual sum of squares, squares each training residual and adds the results. It is the total squared training error, so its value depends on the number of cases and the response units. Compare it on the same observations.

TSS, the total sum of squares, measures response variation around the mean. It is the squared error of a reference predictor that always returns that mean. It describes the data’s variation before asking how much the fitted model explains.

RSE, residual standard error, estimates the spread of the model’s unexplained random error, in response units. For a simple line it is the square root of the whole ratio RSS/(n − 2), allowing for fitting the intercept and slope. It is a training-based estimate of error spread, rather than a test-set prediction score.

R², the coefficient of determination, compares the model’s squared error with TSS. A value of 1 means no residual error, 0 means the same squared error as the mean-only baseline, and a negative value means worse than that baseline. The score requires nonzero TSS. It is not a percentage of predictions that are correct.

Test RMSE, root mean squared error, measures the size of prediction mistakes on observations not used to fit or choose the model. Square the test errors, average them and take the square root. This returns the score to response units and gives larger misses more influence. RSE describes estimated training noise; test RMSE assesses the fixed model’s prediction on new cases.

Ordinary least squares (OLS) estimates coefficients by minimizing RSS. Use validation to compare model choices and a final test set to assess the selected procedure. Underfitting misses a stable pattern, while overfitting follows training details that do not transfer. Repeating training on new samples lets us study model variability.

Train again on new data: does the prediction move?

Bias is the systematic difference between the average learned relationship and the true relationship. At a fixed input, compare the average fitted prediction across repeated training datasets with the true mean response. For example, a straight-line model cannot capture a genuinely quadratic mean over the input range, so its average fit can miss that pattern.

Variance describes how much the learned model changes across possible training datasets sampled from the same population, while keeping the fitting procedure and sample size fixed. At one input, this is the spread of predictions across those fitted models. It describes variation across repeated training, rather than the noise of one fresh observation. A model can have low variance and still be consistently off target.

The noise ε is the observation’s deviation from the true mean, whereas a residual compares an observed value with a fitted model prediction.

At a fixed input x, consider a new observation y and the prediction f(x;𝒟) learned from a random training dataset 𝒟. The expectation 𝔼𝒟,ε averages over both repeated training and noise in a new observation. This is expected mean squared error (MSE), rather than the error of just one observed case.

New observation: y is an outcome not used for training. Its ε is the random difference from the true mean at x. For example, another crop trial with the same fertilizer dose can give a different yield. This random error belongs to the new observation and is independent of the dataset used for training. It is not a training residual. Both sides of the displayed identity give the expected MSE: the right-hand side splits it into three contributions. MSE is the quantity inside the RMSE square root. Here the expectation is at one fixed input, rather than an empirical average over a finite test set.

Self-check

At one fixed input, the true mean is 2. Across repeated training samples a method predicts 1 or 3 equally often. Which description is correct?

  1. Zero bias and zero variance: averaging to 2 makes every fit correct.
  2. Zero bias but nonzero variance: the mean prediction is right, while individual fits move.
  3. Nonzero bias but zero variance: each fitted prediction differs from 2.
  4. Both bias and variance are zero once fresh response noise is removed.
Show answer and reasoning

Answer B. The average prediction is 2, so bias is zero. Predictions differ from their mean by ±1, giving variance 1. The method can be unbiased without each fitted prediction being exact.

A slope test asks about linear association

A fitted slope can be nonzero just because the sample is noisy. We state two hypotheses to check whether the data provide evidence of a linear association beyond this sampling variation. Before looking at a p-value, we specify the claim to test and the competing possibility. This makes the evidence question precise: how unusual would the observed estimate be under the zero-slope hypothesis? The test does not decide that either hypothesis is certainly true.

The null hypothesis H₀: β1 = 0 says that the population linear mean has zero slope. The two-sided alternative H₁: β1 ≠ 0 allows either a positive or negative slope. A fitted slope will rarely be exactly zero even if the null is true, because estimates vary across samples.

Compare the estimate with its sampling uncertainty instead of comparing it with zero alone. Rejecting this null supplies evidence of a linear association under the assumptions. It does not establish causality. Failure to reject does not prove no relationship, especially if precision is low or the true relationship is nonlinear.

The small plots show illustrative population mean functions, not fitted samples. Under the null, the mean is flat even though individual observations may still be noisy. Under the two-sided alternative, the slope may be positive or negative: the two colored lines show separate possibilities, not two simultaneous fitted lines.

Repeated studies give different slopes: put them on one scale

The second block is a theoretical standardization, not an immediately computable sample statistic. Both the true population slope β1 and the true sampling standard deviation σ are unknown in ordinary observed data. The sample gives their estimates, not these population values themselves. The standardized expression can be evaluated only when the true values are supplied, for example by a simulation. It describes the sampling theory that motivates a practical test after a null slope is proposed and the standard error is estimated.

Imagine repeating the same simple-regression study while keeping its input values, sample size and fitting procedure fixed. The population slope β1 is a fixed unknown number. Each new set of noisy responses gives a different fitted slope βˆ1. Under the classical normal-error assumptions, the distribution of these estimates is 𝒩(β1,σ2). Here σ denotes the true sampling standard deviation of the slope estimate, also called its true standard error.

Subtracting β1 centers the distribution at zero. Dividing by σ measures distance in standard-error units. A value of +1 means one standard error above the true slope, and −1 means one standard error below it. The plots use an illustrative true slope 2 and true standard error 0.4. A fitted slope 2.4 therefore becomes (2.4 − 2) / 0.4 = 1. These illustrative values are separate from the 90-observation example.

Distinguish the slope standard error σ from the response-error standard deviation σε. Their exact relationship in simple regression is:

σ=σε∑i=1n(xi−x¯)2

n is the number of observations, xi is input i, and x¯ is the input mean. Equivalently, the slope variance is the response-error variance divided by the centered input sum of squares. Exact under a correct linear mean, varying fixed inputs, and independent normal errors with common variance. The response errors have conditional mean zero, their variance is finite and common across cases, the inputs are not all equal, and inference requires n > 2. In practice σ is unknown. We estimate it on the next slide.

Estimate how much the fitted slope would change

The true standard deviation of the fitted slope across repeated samples is σ. We cannot observe it directly, so we calculate its estimate σˆ from one dataset. This is the same quantity often written SE(βˆ1). It describes uncertainty in the estimated slope, not the scatter of a single response.

The first formula has a rise-over-run intuition. RSE is vertical response noise, measured in response units. The square root of the centered input sum of squares measures horizontal input spread. Dividing converts the estimated vertical noise into uncertainty about the line’s slope, in response units per input unit. This is an intuition for the slope standard-error formula, rather than the ordinary rise-over-run calculation of the fitted slope.

The hat in σˆ means that the standard error is estimated. It is not σ2, and it is not the realized estimation error βˆ1−β1 of this particular fitted slope. It estimates the standard deviation of fitted slopes across hypothetical repetitions of the same model and sampling conditions. Smaller values mean less sampling variability in estimating the true population slope.

First estimate the response-error standard deviation σε with RSE. Then divide by the square root of the centered input sum of squares. If the observed inputs cover a wider range, the data provide more information about the tilt of a line.

Next substitute RSE² = RSS / (n − 2). The denominator n − 2 reflects that the intercept and slope were estimated from the same observations. Finally, expand RSS as the sum of squared residuals. The three displayed formulas are equal, rather than three alternative methods.

The sum over i runs over all n cases. For case i, xi is the observed input, yi the observed response, and yˆi its fitted prediction. x¯ is the mean input. Larger response noise increases the estimated slope uncertainty, while larger input spread or more observations can reduce it, all else equal. The residual degrees of freedom must be positive, n > 2, and the inputs must vary.

Why test a slope before trusting the upward trend?

The preceding slide introduced 16 simulated adult height–weight observations. Fitting a line to this sample gives slope 0.6 kg per cm. An extra centimetre of height is therefore associated with an extra 0.6 kg in the fitted mean weight, not an exact 0.6 kg difference between every pair of people. An upward sample line alone does not tell us whether the population slope is nonzero: sampling noise can produce a nonzero fitted slope even under a flat population mean.

The slope test temporarily assumes the zero-slope null H0:β1=0. We imagine repeating the study under that model and the same sampling setup, then ask how unusual a fitted result at least as far from zero as the observed one would be. If it would be very unusual under the zero-slope model, the data provide evidence against that model under the regression assumptions.

This is a conditional assessment of the observed result. It does not calculate the probability that the population slope is zero, and the hypothetical repetitions are a reference model rather than extra data we must collect. The next comparison holds the fitted slope, sample size and inputs fixed while changing residual noise, showing why uncertainty must enter the comparison. Evidence of a linear association does not establish causation.

For the sample just shown, the fitted slope is 0.6 kg per cm. This sample estimate is not automatically the population slope.

The t statistic measures how far the slope is from zero

Zero is subtracted because it is the population slope proposed by the null hypothesis. We temporarily assume the true slope is zero and assess how unusual the observed fitted slope would be under that assumption, relative to its estimated standard error. The numerator βˆ1−0 is the signed distance between the slope fitted to our data and the zero slope being tested. It is positive above zero and negative below zero. Its absolute value gives distance regardless of direction.

The t value tells us how much the observed slope from our data deviates from a hypothetical zero slope, measured in estimated standard errors. The observed value tobserved is fixed after fitting our dataset. Across hypothetical repetitions under the zero-slope null, the same calculation is random and is denoted T. This test does not assign a probability to the true population slope being zero.

The null hypothesis is H0: β1=0. Compare the fitted slope with that null value, then divide by its estimated standard error σˆ. The observed ratio t counts how many estimated standard errors the slope is from zero. Its sign indicates the direction, while its absolute value measures distance.

If the null and the classical assumptions hold, the random ratio from repeating the entire study follows Student’s t distribution with n − 2 degrees of freedom. Before data are observed this is a random quantity. Once a dataset is fitted, t is one realized value. We use the distribution to judge that realized value. Student’s t is symmetric and centered at zero, with heavier tails than the normal distribution. Estimating the unknown spread introduces the extra uncertainty that this reference distribution accounts for.

For the 90 displayed observations, the slope is 0.1075379991 and its estimated standard error is 0.0179471258. Their ratio is 5.991934335, on 88 degrees of freedom. The number 5.99 is a distance in standard-error units, not a probability. A large absolute t is evidence to investigate against the zero-slope null, not proof of a causal effect. A p-value compares our observed t with a zero-slope reference

Assuming the true slope is zero, the p-value is the probability that a hypothetical repetition of the study gives a t value at least as far from zero as the t value we computed from our original data. These are hypothetical repetitions under the same model and sampling setup, not additional data we must collect. Compare absolute t values, rather than raw slopes.

The two quantities use the same t-statistic calculation but refer to different datasets. T is a random statistic across hypothetical repeated samples under the zero-slope null. tobserved is the fixed number already calculated from our actual dataset. The probability expression asks how often |T|, a random distance, is at least |tobserved|, our observed distance. The vertical bar means “assuming” or “given” H0.

Absolute value means distance from zero regardless of sign. In the illustrative case tobserved=2.2, the outcomes counted are T≤−2.2 or T≥2.2. An equally distant negative result is just as extreme as a positive one. “More extreme” does not mean a larger raw slope without regard to its standard error: it means a t statistic at least as far from zero.

A smaller p-value means that, if the true line were flat, a result like ours or more extreme would be rarer. Thus a small p-value is stronger evidence against a flat population line, under the regression assumptions. It does not give the probability that the population slope is zero, measure effect size, or establish causation. The p in p-value is unrelated to polynomial degree p.

If tobserved=0 and the estimated slope standard error is positive, the fitted slope is zero and the fitted line is flat. Every possible absolute random t value is at least zero, so the two-sided p-value is 1. This gives no evidence against the zero-slope null; it does not prove the population slope is zero.

Choose a significance threshold α before inspecting the result. The often-used value 0.05 is a convention whose appropriateness depends on the task and field. Reject the null when the p-value is below that threshold. Otherwise there is insufficient evidence to reject it, rather than proof of no relationship. A nonlinear relationship is not ruled out by failure to reject a zero linear slope.

A confidence interval adds an uncertainty margin to the slope

Optional reading: this extends the slope test with confidence intervals. It is retained here as additional explanation and is not a separate slide in the main lecture.

A fitted slope is a point estimate. A confidence interval adds a sampling-uncertainty margin around that estimate. For a two-sided 95% interval, multiply the estimated slope standard error σˆ by the Student t critical value t0.975,n−2, then subtract and add the resulting margin to βˆ1.

The critical value is the 97.5th percentile: 97.5% of the reference probability is to its left and 2.5% to its right. Symmetry leaves another 2.5% below the negative critical value, so 95% lies between the two cutoffs. For n = 90 the degrees of freedom are 88 and the multiplier is about 1.987. Thus 0.107538 ± 1.987 × 0.0179471 gives approximately [0.0719, 0.1432].

LINE (1/2): the mean and independent errors

LINE is a memory aid for four assumptions of classical linear regression: L stands for Linearity, I for Independence, N for Normality, and E for Equal variance. These assumptions describe the modeled mean and the errors. They matter because classical standard errors and p-values rely on them to describe uncertainty in fitted coefficients. If the assumptions fail, the usual uncertainty calculations can be misleading. A small p-value or high R² cannot establish these assumptions.

Write the response as the modeled mean plus an unobserved error. εi is the true response minus the population mean specified by the model, for observation i. It differs from the residual, which subtracts the fitted sample prediction. X denotes the observed feature matrix, with one row per observation. Conditional notation means that we fix those feature values when describing the errors.

L means that the conditional mean is a weighted sum of the selected features, with coefficients entering linearly. A polynomial uses powers of the input as features and can still satisfy this requirement. The zero conditional error mean is E[εi|X]=0. If the chosen features omit a real curve, averaging more observations does not remove that systematic mismatch.

I means independence of the underlying errors across observations, conditional on the predictors. Repeated measurements on the same person, time series or shared group effects can create dependence. Treating such related cases as independent can misstate uncertainty. Independence does not require the predictor values to be evenly spaced.

A separate identifiability requirement for unique coefficients is full column rank: no feature column can be written as a combination of the other feature columns. For example, including both height in centimeters and exactly the same height in meters makes those columns redundant. Least-squares solutions can still exist when this condition fails, but individual coefficient values need not be unique.

LINE (2/2): the shape and spread of errors

N and E describe the distribution of the unobserved errors after accounting for the mean response. Normality is a conditional assumption at each fixed input. It does not require the predictor values or the raw responses across all inputs to form a bell curve. N describes distribution shape, while E describes whether its spread changes across inputs.

Normality means errors at each input have a normal distribution centered at zero. Under a correctly specified mean, independent normal errors, equal variance and a full-rank design, the usual finite-sample t tests and confidence intervals are exact. OLS itself can minimize the sum of squared residuals without normal errors.

Equal variance means Var(εi|X)=σε2 for every case. σε2 denotes the common error variance, and Var is the variance operator. This condition is called homoscedasticity. Unequal variance is called heteroscedasticity. A fan-shaped residual cloud is one possible clue. With unequal variance, the usual homoscedastic standard-error formula may misstate uncertainty even if the mean model is appropriate.

The four illustrations are schematics, not data from a particular study. True errors are unobserved, so we use fitted residuals as diagnostic clues. Residual plots can reveal curvature, changing spread or patterns over observation order. A normal Q–Q plot checks distribution shape. Fitted residuals are generally dependent because of fitting constraints, even if the underlying errors are independent. A featureless plot does not prove all assumptions.

N describes the shape of the errors. E describes their spread. Both refer to errors at fixed input values.

N · Normality

At each input, errors follow a normal, bell-shaped distribution centered at zero.

Intuition: Small positive and negative errors are common. Large errors in either direction are rare.

Why it matters: Together with the other assumptions, this supports exact small-sample t tests.

E · Equal variance

Errors have the same spread at every input value.

Intuition: Predicting a larger response should not automatically mean a wider cloud of errors.

Why it matters: Unequal spread can make the usual standard errors unreliable.

Residual plots suggest the next question

Use residual plots to choose a follow-up question. A curved mean pattern suggests checking the conditional mean and suitable transformations. A widening spread suggests investigating variance or scale. Ordered runs suggest temporal or sampling dependence. An isolated point warrants a data-quality and sensitivity check.

Normality needs a different diagnostic, such as a normal Q–Q plot, and visual patterns are evidence rather than automatic verdicts. Fitted residuals are not the independent errors in the model; the fitting constraints generally make residuals dependent. The goal is to investigate the underlying mean and error process.

A normal Q–Q plot compares residual quantiles with the quantiles of a normal distribution. Approximate alignment along a straight reference line supports a normal-shape approximation, while systematic deviations suggest a mismatch. Residual plots remain diagnostic clues rather than proofs.

A defensible regression workflow

Start with a prediction or association question, an observation unit, relevant response units and the information available at prediction time. Choose a split representing the intended use; fit a candidate family with a reliable least-squares solver; compare validation performance and inspect residuals.

After fixing the procedure, assess it on held-out test cases. Report uncertainty together with its assumptions. Keep four claims distinct: fit to observed data, prediction on new cases, evidence about coefficients and evidence of causality. No one score establishes all four.

Reason before calculating

  1. For n training cases, how many coefficients does a degree-5 polynomial have, and what is its full-rank RSE denominator?
  2. Can a correctly calculated model score have negative R² on unseen test observations?
  3. If the feature matrix X has redundant columns, can an ordinary least-squares solution still exist?
  4. Does a slope p-value below 0.05 establish that ice-cream sales cause shark attacks?

Use definitions to justify each answer: count coefficients including the intercept; identify the baseline in R²; distinguish existence from uniqueness in least squares; and separate association from causality. The worked answers that follow explain each distinction.

Explain the distinctions

A degree-5 polynomial has six coefficients, β0 through β5, and full-rank RSE denominator n − 6. This requires n > 6. Held-out R² can be negative when its RSS exceeds the TSS of the held-out mean baseline.

A singular XᵀX means the inverse formula is unavailable and the coefficient minimizer may not be unique; least-squares solutions still exist. A pseudoinverse gives a minimum-norm one. A small association p-value does not establish that changing ice-cream sales would change attack outcomes; common causes and study design remain relevant.

RSE denominator: n − 6, if n > 6 and rank is full.

02 · Baseline

Yes. Its predictions can be worse than the mean-response baseline on that held-out dataset.

03 · Existence

Least squares still has solutions, but coefficients may tie. A pseudoinverse chooses the solution with the smallest coefficient-vector length.

04 · Causality

No. The test concerns association under assumptions. Confounding and study design remain relevant.

Fit carefully. Evaluate separately. Interpret precisely.

Least squares chooses coefficients by minimizing squared residuals. Polynomial features preserve linearity in coefficients. Degree controls the candidate family; validation compares choices and the final test assesses the fixed procedure.

Repeated samples make fits vary. Bias–variance reasoning describes this variation, and standard errors, tests and intervals quantify coefficient uncertainty under assumptions. Multiple predictors change conditional comparisons. Residual diagnostics guide model checks, and association remains distinct from causality.
