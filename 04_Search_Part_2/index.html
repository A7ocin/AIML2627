Heuristic DFS: order siblings, then go deep

Local ordering
h(end(p₁)) ≥ … ≥ h(end(pₘ))
  • p₁, …, pₘ are the m new paths; i indexes them; end(pᵢ) is the endpoint. Keep a LIFO frontier.
  • Push p₁, …, pₘ in this order: largest h first.
  • Pop pₘ next: smallest h is on top.
  • Explore the chosen subtree before older alternatives.
Compare with greedy search

Greedy: choose the best endpoint across the entire frontier.

Heuristic DFS: order the newest siblings, then follow the stack.

It retains DFS’s memory advantage and its lack of a general optimality guarantee.

Search · Heuristic requirements

Two properties to check for A* heuristics

h estimates the cheapest cost still needed to reach a goal. At a goal, use h = 0.
These two properties answer different questions about that estimate.

Admissibility

Does the estimate ever guess too high?

It must never exceed the true cheapest remaining cost. Guessing too low is allowed.

If the cheapest remaining cost is 6:
h = 4 is safe; h = 8 is too high.

Why it matters: supports finding a cheapest solution, provided A* keeps better routes available.

Consistency

Do neighboring estimates agree?

After a move, the estimate must not drop by more than the cost of that move.

If a move costs 1:
3 → 2 passes; 5 → 2 drops too much.

Why it matters: with correct cost updates, graph A* only needs to expand each state once.

Consistency is stronger: with h = 0 at goals, consistency also gives admissibility.
An admissible heuristic can be inconsistent; graph A* may then need to expand a state again.

These are properties of h. Selecting the smallest score, testing goals on selection, and keeping cheaper routes are rules of the algorithm.

Handout explanation ↗Link to this slideAdmissibility in detail →Consistency in detail →
This is an overview before the detailed admissibility and consistency slides. The lecture uses nonnegative estimates and nonnegative edge costs. Admissibility means h never exceeds the true cheapest remaining cost at any state. Consistency means h(u)≤c(u,v)+h(v) on every directed edge, together with h(goal)=0. The right-hand numbers check one edge only, not the entire heuristic. Consistency implies admissibility for states with reachable goals by chaining the inequalities along a route. Admissibility does not imply consistency. Tree A* supports optimality with admissibility and goal tests on minimum-f selection; graph A* must preserve improvements and reopen states when necessary. With consistency and sound best-cost bookkeeping, the first selected route to a state has its cheapest g, so reopening after expansion is unnecessary. The algorithm's selection and duplicate-handling rules are distinct from heuristic properties. Completeness assumptions remain on the following admissibility slide.

Admissible means “never overestimate”

h is estimated remaining cost; h* (“h star”) is the true cheapest remaining cost.

The rule

0 ≤ h ≤ h*

At every state.
At a goal: h = 0.

The true remaining cost is 6. Estimates of 4 and 6 are admissible because they do not exceed 6; 8 is too high.
Why it matters: admissibility keeps estimates optimistic, so A* does not overlook a cheaper solution.

Handout explanation ↗Link to this slide
The chart illustrates remaining costs at one state: the true cheapest remaining cost h* is 6. Estimates h=4 and h=6 are admissible at that state; h=8 overestimates. A heuristic is admissible only if the condition holds at every state. The original notation h(n) and h*(n) makes the state n explicit; the diagram suppresses n for readability. At a goal h=0. For a path reaching the state with g=3 already paid and h=4, f=g+h=7 lower-bounds its cheapest completion cost 3+6=9, and hence every completion through that path. A* tree search selects minimum f and tests goals on selection. With finite branching, an admissible nonnegative h, every edge cost at least epsilon>0 (one fixed positive minimum), and a reachable finite-cost goal, it is complete and cost-optimal. Consistency: check how the estimate changes in one move

If a move costs 1, the estimated remaining cost may drop by at most 1.
Check this rule on every directed edge, from the state before the move (u) to the state after it (v).

Passes this edge

uh(u) = 3
move costs 1→
vh(v) = 2

The estimate falls from 3 to 2: a drop of 1, matching the move cost.

3 ≤ 1 + 2 · consistent on this edge

Fails this edge

uh(u) = 5
move costs 1→
vh(v) = 2

The estimate falls from 5 to 2: a drop of 3, although the move costs only 1.

5 > 1 + 2 · inconsistent on this edge

h(u) ≤ c(u,v) + h(v)h estimates remaining cost; c(u,v) is the cost of the move u → v.
Read it as: “the estimate here ≤ one move’s cost + the estimate after that move.”

Consistency is stronger than admissibility: passing every edge, with h(goal) = 0, guarantees no overestimation. An admissible heuristic can still fail an edge check.

Handout explanation ↗Link to this slide
The two examples are local consistency checks, independent of the course tree. Both use a directed edge with cost 1 and successor estimate h(v)=2. At u, estimate 3 passes and estimate 5 fails. Passing one edge does not certify the whole heuristic. These examples alone do not establish admissibility; that compares each estimate with the true cheapest remaining cost. For a reachable goal, chaining consistency inequalities along a route with h(goal)=0 gives h(start) at most that route's cost, and thus at most the cheapest route cost. Under the course's nonnegative heuristic convention, this establishes admissibility. The course tree's A→B edge uses h(A)=5, h(B)=2 and cost 1, matching the failing case. Handout explanation ↗Link to this slide
The tables follow one path across one edge; g is a path cost, not a fixed property of a state. They use the same heuristic pairs as the preceding slide but assume an accumulated cost of 4 before the move. Consistency requires h_before ≤ edge_cost + h_after. Adding g_before to both sides gives f_before ≤ g_before + edge_cost + h_after = f_after. In full path notation, for path p ending at u and extension p⊕v, f(p⊕v)=g(p)+c(u,v)+h(v)≥g(p)+h(u)=f(p). Search · Worked example

Negative costs can change which route is cheapest

UCS (uniform-cost search) selects the smallest g, the cost already paid. A negative edge can make that cost fall later.

One negative edge can break the stopping rule

A negative loop can remove any minimum

UCS selects the direct goal at 2 before B at 5, but the route via B costs −5. A repeatable negative loop on the way to a goal lowers cost forever: no cheapest solution exists.

Avoiding cycles does not restore ordinary UCS or A* guarantees for negative edges.

Handout explanation ↗Link to this slide
Left graph: A→G costs 2, A→B costs 5, B→G costs −10. UCS selects G at g=2 before B at g=5, but A→B→G costs −5. 03 · Informed search

Branch-and-bound: improve, then prune

  1. B is the pruning bound; initially B = ∞ (infinity: no finite bound).
  2. Explore paths depth first.
  3. p is a path; end(p) its endpoint.
    Prune if its lower bound g(p) + h(end(p)) ≥ B.
  4. At a better goal, save its path and set B = g(p).
  5. Continue until no candidate remains.
What makes pruning safe
  • The estimate must be a lower bound.
  • The incumbent is the best solution found so far.
  • The first solution is not necessarily the best.
  • DFS saves frontier space; safe pruning alone does not guarantee termination.
Search · Read the mathematics

The bound changes; the depth-first loop continues

Use nonnegative costs and 0 ≤ h(v) ≤ h*(v); h* is true least remaining cost.
F: frontier stack · p: popped path · vₛ: start · vG: goal · v: successor · E: legal edges.

F ← stack(⟨vₛ⟩); B ← ∞; best ← none
while F ≠ ∅:
    p ← pop(F)
    if g(p) + h(end(p)) ≥ B: continue
    if end(p) = vG:
        best ← p; B ← g(p)
    else:
        for v with (end(p), v) ∈ E:
            if v is not in p: push(F, p ⊕ v)
return best

Reject an unpromising path

g: path cost; h: remaining estimate.
g + h ≥ B: skip this path.
continue starts the next loop iteration.

Save evidence of improvement

best holds a complete route.
B holds its cost.
A goal updates both; it does not stop the search.

pop removes the latest path; push adds one. p ⊕ v appends v; ∈ means “in”; ← assigns; ∅ means empty.
Check path cycles; return best when the stack is empty. ∞ means infinity; none means no saved route.

Handout explanation ↗Link to this slide
This is the source branch-and-bound rule expressed as executable-style mathematical pseudocode. Assume finite heuristic values; nonnegative costs and admissibility make cycle pruning safe for finding one optimum. The initial bound is infinity and no incumbent exists yet. On a finite graph, only finitely many simple paths exist, so this version terminates. Infinite spaces and a finite initial strict bound require the additional discussion on the following slides.

Putting it together

Choose from the problem’s requirements

Fewest steps · equal positive costs

Try BFS when its frontier fits in memory.
Try IDS when depth is unknown and memory is tight.

Weighted costs · no useful h

Use lowest-cost-first with nonnegative costs and correct duplicates. Check its termination assumptions.

Weighted costs · useful lower bound

Use A* when you can afford its frontier.
Check admissibility, goal tests on selection, and reopening when needed.

Memory tight · optimization needed

Consider depth-first branch-and-bound.
Safe lower bounds, early saved solutions and termination conditions matter.
